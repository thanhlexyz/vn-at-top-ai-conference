Research directions: Khoa D. Doan

Khoa D. Doan (VinUni) · All professors

Work centers on the security of deep learning models, mainly backdoor and adversarial attacks and defenses, alongside language-model training and federated learning. Work on backdoor attacks, including clean-label backdoors from target-class data only, and randomized and patch-based defenses across image, language, federated, molecular and 3D-scene settings. Work on aligning language models with preference optimization and verifiable-reward reinforcement learning, and on reasoning-model behavior. Work on attributing concepts to model parameters in text-to-image diffusion models, then editing them at inference time to erase or amplify a concept.

Open challenges. Work on backdoor attacks and defenses leaves open evaluation against only older defense strategies, retry attacks on randomized defenses, and countermeasures still to be built. Work on reward over-optimization leaves open how to set key hyper-parameters, assumptions about preference data, and generalization beyond mathematics. Work on parameter-level concept editing leaves open how well it generalizes beyond early diffusion models and how cleanly it removes a concept without side effects.

Trustworthy AI (15 papers · CSRankings score 3.15)

Topic description

Papers in this topic study backdoor attacks, where poisoned training data plants a trigger-activated misbehavior, and defenses against backdoor and adversarial attacks. An attack on pretrained 3D Gaussian splatting scenes injects optimized low-opacity Gaussians so that chosen target content appears clearly from one target viewpoint while staying invisible from other viewpoints. For molecular graph neural networks, an admission-aware backdoor builds chemically feasible motif attachments that pass parsing and sanitization checks, keeping high attack success with fully admitted poisons. A clean-label physical backdoor attack frames small perturbations of a few target-class samples as dataset distillation, surpassing dirty-label baselines on facial-recognition and animal-classification datasets. Two papers automate physical backdoor dataset creation with generative models by suggesting trigger objects, synthesizing or editing poisoned candidates and selecting the most plausible ones. For attackers who only supply target-class data, clean-label backdoor attacks select hard target-class samples to poison using a pretrained model or an out-of-distribution dataset, reaching high attack success while bypassing several representative defenses. Two papers make backdoors durable against fine-tuning defenses: one reformulates backdoor training as continual learning with a sharpness-aware minimizer, the other exposes the backdoor to a wider range of learning rates and clean-data epochs. A federated-learning backdoor jointly learns a stealthy trigger and poisons the parameters least updated by the main task, bypassing existing defenses with a more durable effect. A backdoor with a class-conditional trigger generator lets the adversary choose any target class at inference, bypassing defenses designed for single-target backdoors. A defense for vision transformers processes patches before the positional encoding to mitigate patch-based and blending-based backdoor triggers. Lightweight defenses against query-based black-box adversarial attacks randomize the latent representation of text inputs at inference, or add random noise to the hidden features of deep networks, with minimal accuracy loss and no adversarial training. A sparse watermark marks only a small subset of generated tokens, anchored to words with specific part-of-speech tags, giving high detectability and better text quality than earlier watermarks.

HiddenSplat: Injecting View-Specific Content into 3D Gaussian Splatting via ``Invisible'' Gaussians ICLR 2027 · rejected
The problem
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) represents a scene as many 3D Gaussians and renders photorealistic novel views; because 3DGS scenes are shared and reused as assets, the paper studies the security risk that an adversary can hide content inside a pretrained scene.
The idea
The attack injects a set of structurally optimized Gaussians into a pretrained 3DGS scene so that chosen target content (an image, logo, watermark or message) appears clearly from a chosen target viewpoint while staying visually imperceptible from other viewpoints. According to the reviews, the original scene is frozen and low-opacity Gaussians are placed along the target camera's rays, trained with target-reconstruction, non-target preservation, spatial-repulsion and color-dispersion losses.
Strengths
The abstract reports that the attack works in both white-box and gray-box settings, lets the attacker choose the target viewpoint arbitrarily, needs no auxiliary encoder or decoder, and achieves high-fidelity target reconstruction at the attack viewpoint across diverse scenes and target patterns while the content stays invisible from non-target views.
What the authors leave open
  • The authors state that robust and efficient defenses against the attack remain an important direction for future work.
  • The authors state that the autonomous-driving and robotics examples are potential implications only; evaluating end-to-end downstream effects on such systems is beyond the scope of the work.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • The paper demonstrates visual imperceptibility from non-target viewpoints, but does not establish whether the injected content remains difficult to detect when a defender uses AI-based analysis of the complete 3DGS representation.
  • The paper does not clarify whether the comparisons with other methods were conducted under equivalent conditions—specifically, using the same number of Gaussians and the same computational budget.
  • The released pipeline does not support interpreting the test-view results as held-out generalization.
  • The experiments lack direct comparisons with simpler strategies, such as random depth sampling or uniform distribution, making it difficult to fully justify the necessity of the core design elements.

Rethinking Molecular Graph Backdoors under Chemistry-aware Admission NeurIPS 2026 · accepted
The problem
Backdoor attacks on molecular graph neural networks (GNNs), where an attacker poisons training data so the model misbehaves on inputs carrying a trigger, are usually evaluated as abstract graph edits, although real molecular pipelines only accept records that pass parsing, sanitization and consistency checks.
The idea
The paper formalizes an admission protocol that admits a molecular record only if its string is sanitizable and the graph rebuilt from it matches the submitted graph, then proposes an admission-aware attack that builds chemically feasible motif attachments and ranks admitted candidates by fingerprint-based Tanimoto similarity to clean target-class molecules, without access to a victim model.
Strengths
The abstract reports that many existing graph backdoors lose much of their efficacy under the admission checks, while the proposed attack reaches high success with fully admitted poisons and preserves clean accuracy across benchmarks, validators, architectures and defenses.
What the authors leave open
  • The authors note that their chemistry diagnostics support descriptor alignment on one benchmark without establishing synthetic accessibility or biological equivalence of the poisoned molecules.
  • The authors report that post-hoc and model-level defenses reduce attack success but do not remove the attack once poisons enter training.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • ChemGuard may be too minimal to fully support the paper’s claim of realistic chemistry-aware admission.
  • Passing sanitization and graph-string consistency does not necessarily mean that the generated molecules are synthetically plausible, biologically meaningful, or realistic as third-party molecular submissions.
  • Runtime and resource overhead are significant compared to the baselines
  • I am not in a position to fully assess whether RDKit (or other) sanitization is a strong or weak proxy for real molecular ML pipeline constraints.

Clean-Label Physical Backdoor Attacks with Data Distillation AAAI 2026 · accepted
The problem
Physical backdoor attacks use real objects in a scene as triggers that activate a backdoor without digital manipulation, but existing ones are dirty-label: the attacker must change poisoned samples' labels to the target label, and the mismatch between image content and label exposes the attack to human inspection.
The idea
The paper introduces a clean-label physical backdoor attack that needs no label manipulation or trigger injection during training: imperceptible perturbations are added to a few target-class samples, framed as a dataset distillation problem with three variants (parameter matching, gradient matching, feature matching) for linear probing and full fine-tuning.
Strengths
The abstract reports that in hard scenarios requiring backdoor generalizability in the physical world the attack surpasses dirty-label baselines, shown on two collected physical backdoor datasets for facial recognition and animal classification; the authors release code and the animal classification dataset.

Wicked Oddities: Selectively Poisoning for Effective Clean-Label Backdoor Attacks ICLR 2025 · accepted
The problem
Clean-label backdoor attacks poison training data without changing labels, but early versions add triggers to a random subset, ignoring that samples contribute unequally; existing selection strategies need the whole labeled training set and training, which is expensive and not always practical.
The idea
The paper studies a threat model where the attacker only supplies target-class data and knows nothing of the victim model or other classes, and proposes strategies that find hard samples within the target class to poison, using a pretrained model or an out-of-distribution dataset.
Strengths
The paper reports high attack success rates compared to baselines on benchmark datasets and that the attacks can bypass several representative defenses.

  • The pretrained model strategy relies on the availability of pretrained models in similar domains, which may not always be accessible in real-world applications.
  • The OOD approach rely on out-of-distribution data but it’s not clear how this dataset could be obtained

Sparse Watermarking in LLMs with Enhanced Text Quality ICLR 2025 · rejected
The problem
Watermarking for large language models embeds a detectable signal in generated text to monitor misuse, but existing methods trade off the quality of the text against how effectively the watermark can be detected.
The idea
The paper presents a sparse watermark that marks only a small subset of generated tokens spread across the text, anchoring the watermarked tokens to words with specific part-of-speech (POS) tags.
Strengths
The abstract reports high detectability and generated text that outperforms previous LLM watermarking methods in quality across various tasks.
What the authors leave open
  • The authors state that limitations of the current method present opportunities for enhancement that they plan to explore in future work.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • While there is a comparison with distortion-free methods in Appendix F, the only measure for text quality is perplexity (PPL), which is insufficient, and SpARK does not demonstrate an advantage over distortion-free methods.
  • If adversaries know that POS tagging is used in the watermarking process, they could easily defeat it by randomly substituting verbs and nouns with synonyms.
  • the experiments mainly focus on three tags (Verb, Noun, Determiner), with limited analysis on why these particular tags were chosen over others beyond their document frequency.
  • but as the substitution rate increases (e.g., 30% or 50%), the detection accuracy declines.

Flatness-aware Sequential Learning Generates Resilient Backdoors ECCV 2024 · accepted
The problem
Backdoor attacks implant a hidden misbehavior into a model, but some fine-tuning defenses can remove the backdoor, mainly because of the catastrophic forgetting property of deep networks.
The idea
The paper analyzes the loss-landscape connectivity between a backdoored and a fine-tuned model, then reformulates backdoor training through continual learning as two tasks: the first learns a backdoored model, the second moves it to a backdoored region resistant to fine-tuning, with a sharpness-aware minimizer to seek flatter backdoor regions.
Strengths
The abstract reports that fine-tuning defenses can push a poisoned model out of the backdoor regions and that the proposed framework generates resilient backdoors, shown through experiments on several benchmark datasets.
What the authors leave open
  • The authors state that their work exposes another backdoor threat against fine-tuning defenses and urge researchers to develop countermeasures for this type of attack.

Fooling the Textual Fooler via Randomizing Latent Representations ACL 2024 · rejected
The problem
NLP models are vulnerable to adversarial attacks that slightly perturb the input; some query-based black-box attacks, which only need access to the model's outputs, query it many times to find the most important parts of an input text to transform.

Understanding the Robustness of Randomized Feature Defense Against Query-Based Adversarial Attacks ICLR 2024 · accepted
The problem
Deep neural networks are vulnerable to adversarial examples; in a black-box setting an attacker with access only to the model's output can generate them by querying (score-based and decision-based attacks).
The idea
The paper proposes a lightweight defense that adds random noise to hidden features at intermediate layers at inference time, supported by theoretical analysis, and finds that selectively adding noise to different parts of the model based on the gradient of the adversarial objective matters.
Strengths
The abstract reports that the defense needs no adversarial training, has minimal impact on accuracy, applies to any pretrained model, and is robust against multiple black-box attacks across diverse architectures.
What the authors leave open
  • The authors state that an empirical robustness comparison of all adversarial defenses is beyond the scope of the paper, whose objective is to study this randomized feature defense theoretically and empirically.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • The randomness enables the attacker to keep submitting the same adversarial examples until by chance, the model misclassifies it.
  • I am not convinced that this approximation is good especially when the added noise or the perturbation is relatively large.
  • The evaluation of the decision-based attack is insufficient and the results are not good.
  • The results are not significantly better than RND, but injecting feature noise requires a careful choice of the layer.

Forget-Me-Not: Making Backdoor Hard to be Forgotten in Fine-tuning ICLR 2024 · rejected
The problem
Backdoor attacks implant a trigger-activated misbehavior into a model, but because of catastrophic forgetting the backdoor can be gradually removed by advanced fine-tuning, including a cyclical-learning-rate scheme reported to mitigate almost all backdoor attacks.
The idea
The paper proposes a training mechanism that coaches the backdoor to be more durable by exposing it to a wider range of learning rates and to clean-data-only training epochs.
Strengths
The abstract reports that backdoored models built this way bypass fine-tuning-based defenses and keep the backdoor effect under long and sophisticated fine-tuning, even when the whole model is fine-tuned end-to-end on another task.
What the authors leave open

  • The intuition behind proposed method is too hauristic.

IBA: Towards Irreversible Backdoor Attacks in Federated Learning NeurIPS 2023 · accepted
The problem
In federated learning (FL), where models are trained on decentralized data, an adversary can implant a backdoor into the global model; prior FL backdoor attacks often assume the adversary controls many clients or knows other clients' data, use visible triggers, and the backdoor effect fades quickly once the adversary leaves training.
The idea
The attack jointly learns a stealthy trigger and gradually implants the backdoor into the global model, selectively poisoning the parameters least likely to be updated by the main task and constraining the poisoned update to the vicinity of the global model.
Strengths
The abstract reports high success rates on MNIST, CIFAR-10 and Tiny ImageNet while bypassing existing backdoor defenses and achieving a more durable backdoor effect than other backdoor attacks.
What the authors leave open
  • The authors list as future directions generalizing the attack to heterogeneous or non-i.i.d. data distributions, exploring alternative triggers, and evaluating it on larger-scale and real-world FL systems.
  • The authors state that robust defense mechanisms against federated learning backdoor attacks still have to be developed.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • IBA is not evaluated against important state-of-the-art defenses like FLAME[3], SparseFed[4], etc.
  • While the attack is titled "Irreversible Backdoor attack", the longevity of the attack is not well studied.
  • the paper lacks comparison with other state-of-the-art attack methods.
  • Based on Figure 2 and table 2, it seems that the method does not do a good job against Krum and RLR.

Defending Backdoor Attacks on Vision Transformer via Patch Processing AAAI 2023 · accepted
The problem
Vision Transformers (ViTs) have less inductive bias than convolutional networks, and the paper investigates their vulnerability to backdoor attacks, a causative attack where poisoned training data plants a trigger-activated misbehavior.
The idea
After finding that ViTs are quite vulnerable to existing attacks and that clean accuracy and backdoor success respond distinctively to patch transformations applied before the positional encoding, the paper proposes a defense for patch-based and blending-based trigger attacks via patch processing.
Strengths
The abstract reports evaluation on CIFAR10, GTSRB and TinyImageNet in which the defense is very successful in mitigating backdoor attacks on ViTs, and presents it as the first defense using a unique characteristic of ViTs.
What the authors leave open
  • The authors state that more sophisticated anomaly detection approaches could improve the detection rate while keeping the false negative rate low, but this is beyond the scope of the paper.

Marksman Backdoor: Backdoor Attacks with Arbitrary Target Class NeurIPS 2022 · accepted
The problem
Earlier backdoor attacks can only make a model misclassify an input toward a single pre-defined target class, which limits the adversary's capability.
The idea
The attack represents the trigger function as a class-conditional generative model and injects the backdoor with a constrained optimization that learns the trigger generator while embedding the backdoor, so the adversary can pick any target class at inference and receive a matching trigger.
Strengths
The abstract reports high attack performance while preserving clean-data performance on MNIST, CIFAR10, GTSRB and TinyImageNet, and that the attack can bypass existing defenses designed against single-target-class backdoors.
What the authors leave open
  • The authors state that the work calls for defensive studies to counter this more powerful but more sophisticated multi-trigger, multi-payload attack.
  • The authors conjecture that training the trigger generator relates to approximating adversarial example generation with decent transferability, and leave a detailed investigation beyond the scope of the paper.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • Only some early defenses are considered.
  • The work is only evaluated on post-training backdoor investigation methods.
  • I would expect that there’s a more convincing discussion about the necessity of the proposed Marksman.
  • Part of the experimental evaluation can be improved, especially as some direct comparisons are not really fair, as the attacker’s capabilities are different (see comments below).

Language models and NLP (8 papers · CSRankings score 1.40)

Topic description

Verifiable-reward reinforcement learning is studied through primacy bias, showing that easy problems sharpen while hard problems expand reasoning. Reasoning models hold a hidden self-critique direction that can be extracted and steered to improve error detection. Verifiable-reward reinforcement learning is shown to lower Pass@k through negative interference and winner-take-all effects, with data curation as a remedy. A token-level preference objective, derived by density-ratio matching, generalises the DPO loss. Over-optimization in direct alignment algorithms such as DPO is mitigated by a clipped importance ratio on the objective. A strong student learns from its own demonstrations evaluated by a weak teacher, generalising better than weak-supervision methods. An adaptive importance-sampling weight against over-optimization in direct alignment avoids extra hyper-parameters.

On the Primacy Bias in RLVR Training NeurIPS 2026 · accepted
The problem
It is unclear whether Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR), which trains language models on tasks whose answers can be checked automatically, can extend reasoning beyond what the base model already encodes; prior findings conflict.
The idea
The paper studies the question through primacy bias (the tendency to over-rely on already-learned representations) and the lazy training regime (the model stays close to a linearization around its starting point). It combines controlled experiments, analysis of released RLVR-tuned checkpoints, and experiments that train separately on easy and hard problems.
Strengths
The abstract reports that RLVR on mixed data sharpens performance on easy problems with only marginal gains on hard ones, that hard problems help most in expanding reasoning, and that prolonged training on them can cause forgetting on easy problems; this is offered as an explanation of earlier conflicting observations.
What the authors leave open
  • The authors state that the analysis is limited to finite evaluation sets and task-specific reward signals.
  • The authors leave extensions to coding RLVR for future work because of the high cost of sandbox-based program execution.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • the strict "lazy" claim is not directly tested via NTK stability measurement in the LLM setting.
  • Another weakness is that the controlled image task may not fully reflect LLM reasoning.
  • The paper also needs stronger statistical evidence.
  • The concept of “difficulty” is not fully consistent throughout the paper.

Decoding the Critique Mechanism in Large Reasoning Models NeurIPS 2026 · accepted
The problem
Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) backtrack and self-verify on their chain of thought (CoT, the written intermediate reasoning), and the paper asks how they detect and recover from their own mistakes, since such behavior should only help if the model can critique itself.
The idea
The paper inserts arithmetic mistakes into intermediate reasoning steps and finds that models often still reach the correct answer without verbalizing a correction (a hidden critique ability). From hidden activations it extracts a critique vector and steers latent representations with it to change error detection.
Strengths
The abstract reports experiments across multiple model scales and families in which steering with the critique vector improves error detection and the performance of test-time scaling at no extra training cost.
What the authors leave open
  • The authors state that future work should investigate whether the hidden critique behavior stems from pre-training or post-training and how it emerges.
  • The authors state that the effect of silent error correction on CoT faithfulness and alignment warrants further study, and that using it to improve verifier-based approaches, self-consistency and self-correction prompting is left to future work.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • It is unclear whether the same vector captures critique of logical, factual, causal, or planning errors.
  • The interpretation "hidden critique" is stronger than what the experiments conclusively establish.
  • The paper does not cleanly separate error detection from error correction.
  • The steering effect on problem-solving accuracy is low.

The Reasoning Boundary Paradox: How Reinforcement Learning Constrains Language Models NeurIPS 2026 · accepted
The problem
RLVR is used to improve the reasoning of large language models, but evidence suggests it can shrink the reasoning boundary: Pass@k, the probability of producing a correct solution within k attempts, can decline.
The idea
The paper analyzes RLVR learning dynamics and identifies negative interference (learning some training problems lowers the likelihood of correct solutions to others) and a winner-take-all effect (problems with high-likelihood correct solutions under the base model are reinforced while low-likelihood ones are suppressed). It then proposes a data curation algorithm that focuses RLVR on low-likelihood problems.
Strengths
The abstract reports theoretical and empirical analysis on multiple mathematical reasoning benchmarks attributing the effect to on-policy sampling, and a notable improvement in Pass@k from the data curation algorithm.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • SELF may preserve Pass@k partly because it performs weaker effective optimization and stays closer to the base model.
  • The greedy-response failure criterion for identifying low-likelihood problems is empirically motivated, but it remains a heuristic proxy.
  • Most experiments focus on mathematical reasoning tasks, making it unclear how broadly the conclusions generalize to other domains.
  • The paper misses important Pass@k-oriented baselines.

TokenRatio: Principled Token-Level Preference Optimization via Ratio Matching ICML 2026 · accepted
The problem
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) models preferences over full sequences although generation is driven by per-token decisions. Existing token-level extensions decompose a sequence-level Bradley-Terry objective, leaving per-prefix optimality implicit.
The idea
Token-level Bregman Preference Optimization (TBPO) posits a token-level Bradley-Terry model and derives a Bregman-divergence density-ratio matching objective that generalises the DPO loss, with two variants: TBPO-Q learns a state baseline, TBPO-A uses advantage normalisation.
Strengths
On instruction following, helpfulness/harmlessness and summarization benchmarks, the abstract reports better alignment quality, training stability and output diversity than sequence-level and token-level baselines. Accepted at ICML 2026.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • The paper does not evaluate a variant where the per-state correction weight is fixed to 1 (i.e., without state-level correction).
  • the paper does not evaluate whether adding a similar baseline head to DPO or TDPO would yield comparable gains.
  • Section 5.1 relies heavily on LLM-as-a-judge models for evaluation.
  • Time complexity: The loss function requires computing probability differences for each generated token (step), which might be expensive.

The problem
Direct alignment algorithms such as DPO are prone to over-optimization, where the model drifts from the reference policy and degrades; the authors attribute this to the mismatch between the offline distribution and the language model policy.
The idea

Weak-to-strong Generalization via Formative Learning from Student Demonstrations & Teacher Evaluation NeurIPS 2025 · rejected
The problem
As LLMs exceed human capability, reliable human feedback is hard to get, so weak-to-strong generalization asks how weaker teacher supervision can elicit a stronger student's full capability; the strong student may overfit to the weak teacher's mistakes.
The idea
The paper shows that learning from weak supervision implicitly regularizes the strong student toward the weak policy, and instead has the strong student produce its own demonstrations, which the weak teacher evaluates, regularizing the student toward its own reference policy (formative learning).
Strengths
The abstract reports that the method significantly outperforms existing weak-to-strong approaches and is more robust under unreliable feedback than contrastive methods such as DPO.
What the authors leave open
  • The authors note that generating data from the strong student is resource-demanding, and that future work will use the strong student for evaluation to avoid generating strong-student data.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • Experiments stop at a 3B parameter student.
  • Evaluation relies on synthetic rewards and GPT-judge metrics, with minimal human or real-world validation.
  • Hyper-parameters are tuned using ground-truth validation labels, breaking W2S premise.
