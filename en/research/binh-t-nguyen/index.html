Research directions: Binh T. Nguyen

Binh T. Nguyen (VinUni) · All professors

Theoretical analysis and principled objectives for modern learning problems, including fine-tuning, optimal transport, robustness, reasoning post-training and 3D reconstruction. Work on post-training for reasoning: why verifiable-reward reinforcement learning narrows solution coverage, and activation interventions that reduce harmful generations. Work on replacing heuristic densification rules in 3D Gaussian splatting with loss-derived scores that place and remove primitives. Work on quadratically regularized optimal transport establishes how closely regularized transport plans can concentrate around the exact optimal map as regularization shrinks.

Open challenges. Work on reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards leaves open generalization beyond mathematics; activation interventions leave open evaluation on more datasets. Work on loss-derived primitive allocation leaves open large-budget performance, where gains narrow and some metrics regress, and generality beyond one sampling-based framework. Work on regularized optimal transport localization leaves open matching upper bounds beyond the affine case and weaker assumptions than compact support and Lipschitz maps.

The Reasoning Boundary Paradox: How Reinforcement Learning Constrains Language Models NeurIPS 2026 · accepted
The problem
RLVR is used to improve the reasoning of large language models, but evidence suggests it can shrink the reasoning boundary: Pass@k, the probability of producing a correct solution within k attempts, can decline.
The idea
The paper analyzes RLVR learning dynamics and identifies negative interference (learning some training problems lowers the likelihood of correct solutions to others) and a winner-take-all effect (problems with high-likelihood correct solutions under the base model are reinforced while low-likelihood ones are suppressed). It then proposes a data curation algorithm that focuses RLVR on low-likelihood problems.
Strengths
The abstract reports theoretical and empirical analysis on multiple mathematical reasoning benchmarks attributing the effect to on-policy sampling, and a notable improvement in Pass@k from the data curation algorithm.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • SELF may preserve Pass@k partly because it performs weaker effective optimization and stays closer to the base model.
  • The greedy-response failure criterion for identifying low-likelihood problems is empirically motivated, but it remains a heuristic proxy.
  • Most experiments focus on mathematical reasoning tasks, making it unclear how broadly the conclusions generalize to other domains.
  • The paper misses important Pass@k-oriented baselines.

Risk-Aware Distributional Intervention Policies for Language Models ICLR 2025 · rejected
The problem
Language models occasionally generate undesirable content such as harmful or toxic text, despite producing text that appears accurate and coherent.
The idea
A two-stage approach that rectifies activations: an ensemble of layer-wise classifiers is trained on activations by minimizing a smooth surrogate of a risk-aware score, and for detected undesirable content, layer-wise distributional intervention policies perturb the attention heads minimally while guaranteeing the intervention is effective with high probability.
Strengths
Benchmarks on several language models and datasets show the method outperforms baselines in reducing the generation of undesirable output.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • A major limitation is the narrow focus on a limited number of datasets.
  • The derivation of the intervention method in this paper is based on the assumption that distributions P and Q follow a normal distribution.
  • It seems that the method may be limited to the LLaMA family of models.
  • The method for determining the crucial hyper-parameter remains ambiguous.

Computer vision and multimodal learning (1 paper · CSRankings score 0.33)

Topic description

Adaptive density control in 3D Gaussian splatting (3DGS), the set of rules that clone, split and prune Gaussians, relies on proxy signals such as position gradients and opacity. The paper differentiates the multi-view reconstruction loss with respect to an auxiliary mass coordinate attached to each Gaussian and uses the resulting score to choose birth sites and removal candidates. Integrated into a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) based 3DGS pipeline, it reports state-of-the-art reconstruction quality on standard benchmarks, with the largest gains under tight Gaussian budgets.

Papers (1)
Birth-Death Structural Learning for 3D Gaussian Splatting NeurIPS 2026 · accepted
The problem
In 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), the quality-efficiency trade-off depends on adaptive density control, whose cloning, splitting and pruning rules use proxy signals such as position gradients, opacity and image-space error rather than answering where new Gaussians reduce the multi-view reconstruction loss and which can be pruned.
The idea
A first-variation birth-death controller: at each structural update the Gaussian population is frozen, an auxiliary mass coordinate is attached to each primitive, and the multi-view loss is differentiated with respect to these coordinates. High-score Gaussians become birth sites and low-score, low-opacity ones are removed; the score is integrated into 3DGS-MCMC.
Strengths
Across standard 3DGS benchmarks, integrating the score into 3DGS-MCMC gives state-of-the-art reconstruction quality, with the largest gains under tight Gaussian budgets.
What the authors leave open
  • The score measures local mass utility at existing parent sites while the backend determines how new capacity is realized, so transfer beyond the MCMC backend is only examined with a replacement of the position-gradient criterion in vanilla 3DGS (Appendix D).
  • Future compact and dynamic radiance fields could bypass heuristic bottlenecks by letting the reconstruction risk govern structural growth.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • At higher Gaussian capacities, the performance gap narrows and occasionally regresses (e.g., LPIPS on Tanks & Temples).
  • it remains entirely unproven whether this variational score functions outside the MCMC framework.
  • Computing the first-variation score adds noticeable computational overhead, making training approximately 1.2 to 1.3 times slower than the 3DGS-MCMC baseline.
  • The main quantitative results are currently reported from a single run.

Optimization methods (1 paper · CSRankings score 0.33)

Topic description

Quadratic regularization of optimal transport adds a squared penalty on the density of the transport plan and yields sparse plans. The paper proves a general lower bound of order epsilon^(1/(d+2)) (epsilon is the regularization strength, d the dimension) on how closely the support of the plan can concentrate around the Monge map, the optimal map of unregularized transport, matching an exponent conjectured in prior work. It also relates the value gap to the mean-squared deviation, derives a sharp upper bound when the map is affine, and checks the scaling on synthetic experiments.

Quadratically Regularized Optimal Transport: Localization Bounds and Affine Case Analysis ICML 2026 · accepted
The problem
Quadratically regularized optimal transport (QOT) gives sparse transport plans, but how fast the support of its optimizer concentrates around the Monge coupling (the optimal map of unregularized transport) as the regularization shrinks had remained open.
The idea
Proves a general lower bound on localization in directed Hausdorff distance, relates the QOT value gap to the mean-squared deviation from the Monge map, and in the affine Brenier regime reduces the problem to self-transport to obtain a pointwise tube bound.
Strengths
The lower bound of order epsilon^(1/(d+2)) matches the exponent conjectured in prior work under standard regularity assumptions; the value gap controls the mean-squared deviation at scale epsilon^(2/(d+2)); a sharp pointwise bound of the same order holds in the affine Brenier regime, including Gaussian-to-Gaussian transport; a synthetic high-dimensional experiment is reported.
What the authors leave open
  • The pointwise upper bound is restricted to the affine Brenier regime; closing the gap between existing general upper bounds and the epsilon^(1/(d+2)) barrier in the fully non-affine case remains open.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • matching pointwise upper bounds are only shown in the affine regime, leaving a gap in the full localization characterization outside special cases.
  • Several assumptions (compact support, density bounded above/below, Lipschitz Monge map) are standard but restrictive for some ML settings (e.g., non-compact Gaussians or heavy-tailed distributions).
  • The experiments are consistent with the theory but remain fairly narrow in scope, as they only address a synthetic affine regime and mainly serve as a sanity check.

Learning theory and cognitive science (1 paper · CSRankings score 0.25)

Topic description

The paper studies why low-rank adaptation (LoRA), which fine-tunes a model by training small low-rank updates on top of frozen weights, suffices, without the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) linearization used by most earlier analyses. It shows that under trace-norm regularization the global minimizers have rank controlled by the rank of a linear map induced by frozen activations rather than by the sample size, with tighter bounds for multi-head attention. It also proves that after a small generic perturbation, first-order stationary points of the factorized objective are rank-deficient above a rank threshold, and gives generalization bounds.

  • The rank-deficiency result is only a first-order statement for perturbed objectives, and spurious local minima can remain. (stated by the authors)
  • Because the optimal solution's rank depends on the optimal solution itself, it is not clear to the reader how to use these results. (raised by reviewers)
  • the linear assumptions used are unrealistic and it is not clear whether the bounds are tight. (raised by reviewers)
Papers (1)
How Finite-Rank Bottlenecks Shape the Low-Rank Adaptation Landscape NeurIPS 2026 · accepted
The problem
Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) is the standard parameter-efficient fine-tuning method, but theoretical explanations for why low-rank updates suffice remain incomplete, and most analyses rely on the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) regime, which linearizes the network around the pretrained weights.
The idea
Observes that with a frozen backbone the loss sees each adapted weight matrix only through a finite-rank linear map induced by the frozen activations. Under trace-norm regularization this gives global minimizers whose rank is set by the rank of that map, with tighter bounds for multi-head attention from softmax and value-projection invariances, and a result on stationary points of the factorized LoRA objective after a small generic perturbation.
Strengths
The paper proves global minimizers whose ranks are controlled by the rank of the frozen map rather than the sample size, derives tighter rank bounds for multi-head attention, shows that every first-order stationary point of the perturbed factorized objective is rank-deficient above a rank threshold, and gives uniform generalization bounds.
What the authors leave open
  • The rank theorems certify the existence of low-rank minimizers for trace-norm objectives, not the convergence of gradient-based LoRA training.
  • The bounds in the main rank theorem are based on global solutions and cannot be calculated explicitly, and the attention bounds are a posteriori.
  • The rank-deficiency result is only a first-order statement for perturbed objectives, and spurious local minima can remain.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • Because the optimal solution's rank depends on the optimal solution itself, it is not clear to the reader how to use these results.
  • The results seem new but they are obtained under specific settings and assumptions that do not exist in practical models.
  • the linear assumptions used are unrealistic and it is not clear whether the bounds are tight.
  • the rank function is known to be difficult to compute/estimate precisely due to its non-continuous nature.

Trustworthy AI (1 paper · CSRankings score 0.25)

Topic description

Wasserstein distributionally robust optimization (WDRO) measures robustness against shifts of the whole data distribution within a Wasserstein ball, rather than single-point perturbations. The paper tightens upper bounds on the worst-case loss for ReLU networks using their piecewise-affine structure on activation cells, and builds an attack that constructs a worst-case distribution. It reports competitive robust accuracy against state-of-the-art baselines and tighter certificates than existing methods.

Papers (1)
Tight Robustness Certificates and Wasserstein Distributional Attacks for Deep Neural Networks ICLR 2026 · rejected
The problem
Wasserstein distributionally robust optimization (WDRO) measures robustness as the worst-case loss over all data distributions within a Wasserstein distance of the data, but existing bounds based on global Lipschitz constants or strong duality are loose or expensive to compute for deep networks.
The idea
The paper takes a primal approach with exact local Lipschitz certificates, using the piecewise-affine structure of ReLU networks on their activation cells, and proposes a Wasserstein distributional attack (WDA) that directly builds a candidate worst-case distribution with a flexible number and location of attack points.
Strengths
The paper reports robust accuracy competitive with state-of-the-art attack baselines and tighter certificates than existing methods, and reviewers note that the evaluation on CIFAR-10/100 shows robust accuracy falling as the parameter κ increases.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • The proposed attack does not appear to outperform existing methods when κ (kappa) is set to 1, and when transformer-based models are included in the experiments.
  • I cannot see any idea of distributional attack here because this requires the interaction of data examples during attack.
  • It is unclear the role of tighter bounds in developing Wasserstein distributional attack.
  • No experiments on ImageNet which is the common standard for papers in adversarial attacks currently.
