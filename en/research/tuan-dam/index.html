Research directions: Tuan Dam

Tuan Dam (HUST) · All professors

Prof. Tuan's papers analyse Monte-Carlo tree search and bandit algorithms, mostly through convergence rates and sample-complexity bounds. Work on tree search with richer value backups for stochastic and continuous-action planning, including return distributions, shared graph nodes, risk sensitivity and non-stationary rewards. Work on best-arm identification with one-bit feedback and top-two sampling, plus variance-driven exploration and conserved quantities of training, with provable guarantees. Work on orthogonal perturbations for sharpness-aware training constrains the weight-matrix perturbation to be approximately orthogonal using Newton-Schulz iterations instead of an SVD.

Open challenges. Work on risk-sensitive tree search leaves open distributional value nodes, learned dynamics, static risk objectives, the overhead of state sharing and broader baseline comparisons. Work on best-arm identification with one-bit feedback leaves open adaptively optimized thresholds, the anchor-point assumption, early-learning validity of variance approximations and weak conservation laws. Work on orthogonalized sharpness-aware minimization leaves open its extra cost, interplay with adaptive optimizers, and whether gains come from the orthogonal perturbation.

Reinforcement learning, planning and agents (13 papers · CSRankings score 6.53)

Topic description

Monte-Carlo tree search backups are replaced by power-mean, optimal-transport, categorical or particle return-distribution, Wasserstein-barycenter and robust worst-case backups, and an exploration bonus for graph search pools evidence across successor states. The papers prove convergence rates such as O(n^-1/2) at the root and sample-complexity bounds for continuous actions, and report gains on stochastic and partially observable benchmarks and Atari games. Other papers speed replanning after reward shifts on small tabular domains and prove a regret bound of order sqrt(T), up to logarithmic factors, for non-stationary reinforcement learning with low-rank drift. A graph-based variant of Monte-Carlo tree search for stochastic Markov decision processes shares states reached at the same planning depth while keeping separate values per depth, with power-mean backups; it converges at O(n^-1/2) at the root for a fixed horizon, matching the tree-based version, and improves sample efficiency over tree-based and graph-based baselines on stochastic planning benchmarks.

Effective-Resistance Exploration in Monte-Carlo Graph Search ICLR 2027 · rejected
The problem
Monte-Carlo graph search shares samples across paths that reach the same state, so an action may have been selected few times even though its successor states were explored through other paths. The paper asks how exploration should respond to this shared evidence in stochastic planning.
The idea
An exploration bonus motivated by effective resistance combines an action's own visit count with the evidence pooled at its successor states, built on graph-based Power-UCT and extended to entropy-regularized graph search.
Strengths
The paper reports conditions under which the rule preserves the O(n^-1/2) convergence rate of the root value estimate for a fixed horizon, and a bound showing how sample sharing reduces cumulative exploration bonuses.
What the authors leave open
  • Proving a planning advantage from shared-successor exploration remains open.
  • Whether clipping substantially affects planning, or an unclipped recursive bonus admits bounds whose constants scale as p_min^-H (p_min is the smallest positive transition probability), remains open.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • 60 pages of proofs with no empirical results besides toy examples
  • Matched comparisons with the same representation and regularizer, including a bonus based only on the local action count, are needed to establish what the successor term contributes.
  • However, the theoretical results establish preservation of existing convergence guarantees rather than an improved convergence rate or sample-complexity result.
  • The experiments do not clearly isolate the contribution of the ER bonus.

What Action Geometry Buys: A Design Space for Optimal-Transport Bellman Backups ICLR 2027 · rejected
The problem
Many reinforcement learning action spaces carry geometry (a notion of similarity between actions) that maximum-entropy backups ignore, and planning over large discrete action sets must branch over all K actions.
The idea
Laplacian optimal-transport (LOT) regularization has a closed-form backup that averages cost-shifted local softmaxes governed by one anchor-normalized action kernel; the paper turns this into a design space that links kernel properties (concentration, cost stability, Poisson truncation, positive couplings) to guarantees.
Strengths
The paper reports exact pruning error and a localized planner whose cost depends on an effective action count instead of K, tabular Q-learning guarantees, and a graph-local kernel that targets the heat-kernel value, with matched obstructions showing these properties are not free.
What the authors leave open
  • The heat-kernel conclusion is restricted to graph-family and accuracy sequences for which the required radius stays in the Gaussian regime and before finite-graph neighborhood saturation.
  • The no-explicit-K-dependence result for the graph-local kernel holds under polynomial growth.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • No evidence of relevance to RL practice: The only experiments are controlled illustrations on synthetic graphs and toy tabular MDPs with known transition kernels.
  • Its correctness cannot be reasonably verified within a conference review, and the 9-page body is not self-contained.
  • Although Theorems 2–3 establish favorable complexity guarantees for localized planning, their practical significance depends on the required localization radius and the associated structural constants.
  • these results do not necessarily imply that geometrically similar actions have similar state-conditional effects or induce comparable action values.

Graph-Based Stochastic-Power-UCT: Monte-Carlo Graph Search with Power Mean Estimation NeurIPS 2026 · accepted
The problem
Tree-based Monte-Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) duplicates a state reached through different trajectories, which can waste simulations in stochastic Markov decision processes (MDPs).
The idea
GS-Power-UCT shares states reached at the same planning depth in a graph while keeping separate values per depth, with power-mean backups; two full-state variants store one node per physical state, one of them with an adaptive horizon.
Strengths
The paper proves an O(n^-1/2) root convergence rate for a fixed horizon, matching tree-based Stochastic-Power-UCT, and reports improved sample efficiency over tree-based and graph-based baselines on stochastic planning benchmarks.
What the authors leave open
  • The power-mean backup assumes rewards in [0, Rmax]; signed rewards would need affine shifts or modified backup operators.
  • With few or no same-depth transpositions, sharing gives little reuse while keeping hash-table and graph-bookkeeping overhead; memory can scale up to |S|·H nodes.
  • Full merging has an evaluator-dependent error bound that need not vanish, and the adaptive-horizon guarantee requires control of horizon-dependent constants.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • I think the assumption is not clearly stated.
  • The runtime overhead can be the important concern.
  • Without comparison to these broader RL baselines, it is difficult to assess whether the method is competitive beyond the MCTS family.

Graph Topology Augmentation for Prioritized Sweeping in Non-stationary Reinforcement Learning NeurIPS 2026 · accepted
The problem
Prioritized Sweeping (PS) orders model-based backups by residual size. After a localized reward shift under a limited replanning budget, residuals propagate only through realized backups, so distant or blocked states can stay idle.
The idea
GTA-PS adds to the residual priority key a mixture of regularized directed-Laplacian potentials, with the topology weight set by a scheduler based on the Second Largest Eigenvalue Modulus (SLEM) of the transition chain.
Strengths
The paper proves that the forward potential coincides with discounted residual propagation at a canonical regularization parameter and reports improved replanning efficiency over vanilla PS on FourRooms and GARNET with DP and Dyna hosts.
What the authors leave open
  • Beyond small-to-medium tabular domains the scaling of the mechanism is unmeasured, and the reward shifts studied are localized and positive.
  • Adversarial, drift-heavy or spark-like transport can distort the topology signal and weaken guidance.
  • Because refreshes and queue maintenance cost real time, the gain is replanning quality under fixed budgets, not lower wall-clock cost; the analysis assumes an exact model, so learned models are future work.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • The paper shows promising results but only compares against standard PS and not any other related work.
  • The authors only evaluated on relatively small tabular MDPs.
  • The scheduler dials topology down exactly where the paper says it helps most.
  • The only evidence that the right states surface is the fairly modest Spearman correlation.

Beyond Expected Values: Risk-Sensitive Planning with Distributional Monte-Carlo Tree Search NeurIPS 2026 · accepted
The problem
Monte-Carlo Tree Search typically stores only scalar mean estimates, which makes it poorly suited to tail-risk objectives such as conditional value-at-risk (CVaR).
The idea
CATSO and PATSO keep finite-support empirical backup laws at Q-edges (categorical atoms with Dirichlet Thompson sampling, or adaptive particles), select actions by the CVaR of a Thompson-sampled law plus a polynomial optimism bonus, and propagate scalar values by visit-weighted averages of child CVaR scores.
Strengths
The paper proves an O(n^-1/2) root value error for a finite-depth nested CVaR target, with an added discretization bias for CATSO and a compression bias for capped PATSO, and reports better lower-tail return and fewer catastrophic outcomes on stochastic planning benchmarks.
What the authors leave open
  • The theory targets a finite-depth nested CVaR, which is not the static CVaR of the full infinite-horizon trajectory return.
  • CATSO depends on a finite grid and so incurs discretization bias, while capped PATSO trades memory for a tunable Wasserstein compression bias.
  • Future work should study distributional V-nodes, adaptive risk levels, learned model dynamics, and partially observed or continuous-control settings.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • The guarantees target the finite-depth nested CVaR objective, which is not the static CVaR of the full trajectory return that practitioners often care about
  • there is no comparison against existing distributional or risk-sensitive MCTS baselines (only scalar UCT-style planners), weakening the practical case for the proposed representations.
  • The fixed-grid analysis also does not cover the adaptive-grid CATSO actually used in experiments, which introduces an unanalyzed projection bias.
  • The theoretical analysis and current methodology are limited to tabular, finite-depth environments, requiring further work to integrate smoothly with deep functional approximations (like AlphaZero-style neural nets).

Second-Order Smooth Planning with Optimal-Transport Bellman Smoothing ICML 2026 · accepted
The problem
Planning with a generative model estimates a state's value with few simulator calls. SmoothCruiser reaches problem-independent complexity O~(eps^-4) by exploiting the smoothness of the entropy-regularized Bellman backup, but its estimator is only first-order.
The idea
The paper shows that the exponent of SmoothCruiser-type planners is set by the order of the local Taylor remainder, and uses an optimal-transport-smoothed Bellman backup with a closed form, a policy gradient, a Lipschitz Hessian and an unbiased cross-product estimator of the quadratic correction.
Strengths
The paper reports O~(eps^-3) oracle complexity for fixed OT parameters (recovering SmoothCruiser at remainder order 2) and relates the OT, entropy-regularized and unregularized objectives through explicit regularization-bias bounds.
What the authors leave open
  • The guarantees are primarily oracle-complexity guarantees for finite action sets and fixed smoothing parameters.
  • For unregularized planning, choosing smoothing parameters as a function of epsilon changes the hidden constants, so the transfer is a regularization-bias tradeoff rather than a parameter-free O~(eps^-3) theorem.
  • The gap-dependent algorithms are only sketched as extensions of the main result.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • In Theorem 7.1, it requires the bounds to be computed exactly, which is only possible for discrete settings?
  • It's very hard to see how to apply any of the theoreticaly results to practice, especially given the computational complexity of the algorithm is very high.
  • It is not clear how the OT-regularized solutions are transferred to unregularized ones, or at least entropy-regularized, which are much more well-studied.
  • The technical routes are not identical, but the connection is close enough that the lack of discussion weakens the positioning of the paper.

Monte-Carlo Tree Search in a d-dimensional continuous action space lacks sample-complexity guarantees that relate dimension and smoothness, which matters for high-dimensional continuous control planning.
The idea
A dimension-adaptive epsilon-net schedule is combined with power-mean backups and a polynomial exploration bonus, with on-demand capped random nets, when the optimal action-value function is beta-Holder continuous.
Strengths
The paper reports that an epsilon-optimal action is found in O~(sigma^2 L^(d/beta) eps^-(d/beta+2)) simulations, matching continuum-armed lower bounds up to logarithmic factors, and that the method outperforms baselines on continuous control planning.

  • To better evaluate the discretization strategy, more high-dimensional environments are needed; only two are provided.
  • since continuous control is often handled with policy-gradient methods (e.g., PPO, SAC), benchmarking against such baselines research would better contextualize practical usefulness.

Distributional Monte-Carlo Tree Search with Thompson Sampling in Stochastic Environments ICLR 2026 · rejected
The problem
Monte-Carlo Tree Search can struggle in highly stochastic domains where transition randomness and partial observability induce underexploration and miscalibrated value estimates.
The idea
CATSO and PATSO keep a return distribution at each Q-node (fixed atoms, or dynamically capped particles with merge-on-insert) and choose actions by a Thompson draw plus a polynomial optimism term.
Strengths
The paper proves non-asymptotic simple regret O(n^-1/2) at the root for both algorithms, connects the tree policies to Wasserstein distributionally-robust MDPs, and reports experiments on synthetic stochastic trees and 12 Atari games.
What the authors leave open
  • V-nodes remain scalar for tractability, and extending distributional backups to V-nodes is a main limitation left for future work.
  • Scaling to larger or partially observed domains and integrating with deep models such as MuZero-style planning or sparse-reward robotics tasks are future directions.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • All proofs and details are deferred to the appendix but the authors did not include an appendix.
  • Typically, we don't want to explore over noise, as this implies risk-seeking behavior.
  • There is no ablation to isolate the contribution of the optimism bonus versus the distributional representation.
  • While the results are promising, especially for environments with stochasticity, they are somewhat mixed compared to baseline approaches.

The problem
Monte-Carlo Tree Search backups for highly stochastic and partially observable MDPs need to propagate the uncertainty of value estimates through the tree.
The idea
Value and action-value nodes are modeled as Gaussian distributions, and a backup operator computes each value node as the Wasserstein barycenter of its action-value children, using an L1-Wasserstein barycenter with alpha-divergence tied to the generalized mean backup, with optimistic and Thompson-sampling selection.

Learning theory and cognitive science (5 papers · CSRankings score 1.32)

Topic description

Best-arm identification in generalized linear bandits is studied when the latent reward is observed only through a thresholded bit, with confidence sets built directly on pairwise reward gaps, an (epsilon, delta)-PAC guarantee, and upper and lower sample-complexity bounds governed by the same instance-dependent hardness up to logarithmic factors. Fixed-confidence best-arm identification with 1-bit feedback builds a time-uniform mean estimator from randomized threshold queries, and proves an anytime PAC guarantee, a gap-adaptive sample complexity and a worst-case lower bound that matches the leading dependence up to logarithmic factors. Non-asymptotic stopping-time bounds with the exact leading coefficient are proved for a top-two rule on Bernoulli bandits. All conserved quantities of gradient flow are characterized for feedforward networks with GELU, SiLU and SwiGLU, multi-head attention and Mixture-of-Experts, using complex-analytic techniques. Samples in pure exploration are allocated to reduce the variance of the final decision through a first-order expansion, with guarantees on variance decay and simple regret for best-arm identification, tree search and best-policy identification, and larger empirical gains in highly stochastic environments.

Best Arm Identification in Generalized Linear Bandits with 1-Bit Feedback ICLR 2027 · rejected
The problem
In applications such as IoT sensor networks and clinical trials, decisions rely on binary feedback. The paper asks how observing a latent exponential-family reward only through a thresholded bit affects best arm identification (BAI) in generalized linear models.
The idea
The algorithm builds confidence sets directly for pairwise reward gaps between arms from the censored likelihood and selects arms using the censored Fisher information along reward-gap directions.

Nearly Optimal Fixed-Confidence Best-Arm Identification with 1-Bit Feedback NeurIPS 2026 · accepted
The problem
In fixed-confidence best-arm identification with 1-bit feedback the learner only learns whether a sampled reward falls in a chosen set, so empirical means are unavailable. This models communication-constrained learning.
The idea
A time-uniform 1-bit mean-estimation primitive uses randomized threshold queries and a clipped tail-integral identity, and is embedded in candidate-challenger BAI algorithms with fixed or phased adaptive clipping.
Strengths
The paper reports an anytime (epsilon, delta)-PAC guarantee for the fixed-clipping algorithm, a gap-adaptive sample complexity for the phased one, and a K-arm worst-case lower bound that matches its leading dependence up to logarithmic factors.
What the authors leave open
  • The algorithms rely on a localization assumption, and developing methods that weaken or remove this requirement is an important next step.
  • The analysis relies on a K-arm worst-case lower bound, leaving the exact instance-dependent complexity of 1-bit BAI open.
  • Extending the time-uniform 1-bit primitive to broader pure-exploration tasks is left as a direction for communication-constrained learning.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • Even the bandwith-constrained communication has caveats as the learner needs to communicate the threshold (which is a real value and thus requires as much bandwith as the reward itself).
  • A simple phased successive-elimination BAI wrapper around Lau–Scarlett’s fixed-target finite-variance 1-bit mean estimator appears to match the leading rate in Theorem 10.
  • knowing an anchor point for each arm seems unrealistic.

Conservation Laws for Modern Neural Architectures ICML 2026 · accepted
The problem
Conservation laws are quantities that stay constant along gradient-flow training. A complete characterization was missing for modern components such as feedforward networks with GELU, SiLU and SwiGLU, multi-head attention and Mixture-of-Experts, and for multi-head attention it was posed as an open problem by Marcotte et al.
The idea
The paper characterizes all conservation laws of these components by expressing them as functions orthogonal to the parameter gradients, reducing the condition to differential constraints that it solves with complex-analytic techniques.
Strengths
The paper reports complete characterizations for feedforward networks with GELU, SiLU and SwiGLU, multi-head attention with sinusoidal and rotary position encoding, and Mixture-of-Experts, and tracks the predicted invariants during training.
What the authors leave open
  • The reliance on complex-analytic techniques requires further refinement to handle two experimental regimes.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • The identified conservation laws are generally weak in the sense that it only defines invariant manifolds of dimensions slightly smaller than the ambient dimension in the parameter space.
  • Most of the nontrivial conservation laws still take the form of norm differences or Gram-matrix differences.
  • Another important question that is not adequately discussed is how to build conservation laws for compositions of the network components studied in the paper.
  • This creates a mismatch between the theorem assumptions and the systems used for validation.

Variance Driven Exploration: A Provable and Efficient Methodology for Pure Exploration in Highly Stochastic Environments ICML 2026 · accepted
The problem
In pure exploration under high stochasticity, optimism-based methods regulate each component's local estimation error without quantifying how that uncertainty affects the final decision.
The idea
Variance Driven Exploration (VarDE) models the final decision as a smooth function of local estimates, decomposes its variance by a first-order Taylor expansion into influence weights times component variances, and greedily samples the highest-impact component; it is applied to BAI, MCTS and best-policy identification.
Strengths
The paper reports theoretical guarantees on variance decay and simple regret for the three problems and consistent empirical improvements over existing methods, with larger gains in highly stochastic environments.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • the paper simplifies the decision variance via a first-order approximation, but it remains unclear how this approximation impacts convergence—either in the theoretical analysis (regret) or in the numerical experiments.
  • The $O(t^{-1})$ rate does not capture VarDE's advantage over baselines
  • The first-order approximation has uncontrolled validity early in learning (Soundness).
  • One limitation is that the theoretical result is uneven across applications.

OrthoSAM: Orthogonal Perturbations for Sharpness-Aware Minimization NeurIPS 2026 · rejected
The problem
Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) seeks flat minima by minimizing the worst-case loss in a neighborhood, but it treats weights as flattened vectors, so its perturbation can be dominated by a few large singular directions of weight matrices.
The idea
OrthoSAM constrains perturbations to a structured manifold with orthogonality properties and uses Newton-Schulz iteration to obtain approximately orthogonal perturbations without a singular value decomposition (SVD).
Strengths
The paper reports improved generalization across architectures and datasets and stronger robustness to label noise; reviewers describe results on CIFAR, Tiny-ImageNet and GLUE plus a PAC-Bayes bound and a convergence analysis.
What the authors leave open
  • OrthoSAM inherits the two-forward-pass cost of SAM and adds two Newton-Schulz projections per iteration.
  • All image-classification experiments use SGD-based training; the interplay with adaptive optimizers such as AdamW, especially in large-scale pre-training, is left for future work.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • Without an ablation study, it is impossible to tell whether OrthoSAM's improvements come from the orthogonalized perturbation or simply from a stronger base optimizer.
  • The authors claim that SAM's ascent step pushes parameters away from flat regions and destabilizes training, yet they provide no empirical or theoretical evidence for this claim.
  • OrthoSAM appears to be essentially a merge of Muon and SAM.
  • Equal-epoch comparisons are not enough because OrthoSAM is substantially slower than standard SAM and several efficient SAM variants.
