Research directions: Xuan-Bach Le

Xuan-Bach Le (HCMUT) · All professors

Prof. Bach's papers study the privacy, safety, evaluation and cost of machine-learning systems, mostly LLM-based agents. Work on memory transfer between language-model agents and on privacy accounting, deletion and action gating, so guarantees survive restarts and model upgrades. Work on routing LLM code to cheaper languages and on pruning long dialogue history while preserving user commitments. Work on synthetic data for African languages tests whether synthetic examples rated highly by an LLM judge actually help classification in low-resource languages.

Open challenges. Work on memory migration between language models leaves open whether risk depends on the model pair, guarantees outside simulators, unmet proof assumptions and unvalidated labels. Work on LLM memory pruning for dialogue lacks a reliable commitment-fidelity benchmark, and leaves open whether code-routing savings survive a terse Python prompt. Work on synthetic data for African languages leaves open whether gains come from quality filtering or set size, ordering beyond three seeds, and GPU cost.

Trustworthy AI (4 papers · CSRankings score 0.90)

Topic description

None of seven privacy accountants in six widely used packages detects a restart from an earlier state, and making the accountant's charge a precondition for releasing each training batch gives, under stated assumptions, an accounted cost that never falls below what was released. Migration-induced safety and privacy inversion of agent memory is formalized over ordered pairs of models with three executable-outcome metrics, two structural results on what a target-agnostic transform can and cannot preserve, a typed memory compiler and a distribution-free selective-migration gate, measured over the full source–target matrix of six open-weight models in a pilot and a confirmatory replicate where the pre-registered gate passes in both runs. Deletion for agent memory is defined counterfactually as behaving like an agent that never saw the data, with a typed artifact-lineage graph and a scoped audit; deleting only the raw record leaves 0.45 excess risk on a synthetic benchmark. Agent actions are gated by risk computed over provenance graphs, reaching AUPRC 91.8 against 74.2 for a content classifier, while the hypothesised causal mechanism is reported as not supported.

Papers (4)
Attested but Unaccounted: DP-SGD Privacy Accounting as a Precondition of Every Step ICLR 2027 · rejected
The problem
Providers of confidential training give customers an attestation report, which proves which software image is running, and a differential-privacy (DP) guarantee (ε, δ) for the released model; the paper argues the report cannot support the guarantee, because ε depends on how many training steps read the data and a correctly attested trainer can be restarted from an old state or skip reporting steps to its privacy accountant.
The idea
DP-ATTEST makes privacy accounting a precondition of every DP-SGD (differentially private stochastic gradient descent) step: a separate data plane releases a step's batch or its noised gradient only against a token, and an attested accountant mints that token only after recording the step's privacy charge in an external register assumed to move only forward.
Strengths
The paper reports that none of seven privacy accountants in six widely used packages detects a restart from an earlier state, that ordinary checkpoint–crash–resume DP-SGD executes 800 steps while accounting for 600, and that rewinding only the accountant buys up to 54 accuracy points on UCI digits at an unchanged published ε; it proves that, under stated assumptions, the accounted privacy cost never falls below the cost of what was released.
What the authors leave open
  • Where the actual privacy loss lies when a shuffling data loader is used is left open: the paper notes that its reported ε column is the accountant's bound at the conservative endpoint, not a measurement of the mechanism that ran.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • The paper supposes that the attacker can only compromise dependency on the training machine. Why can’t they do this on the accountant/data plane/register machine creating the same problem?
  • Several mechanisms required by the proof (external monotone register that the adversary can neither lower nor stall, secret sampler randomness, no live cloning inside a prepaid window) are not provided by current confidential VMs
  • The design has the drawback of unnecessarily accounting for extra privacy in the case that the crash was honest.
  • the experiments do not demonstrate the costs of running on actual confidential VMs or GPUs.

Harmless Here, Harmful There: Safety and Privacy Inversion of Agent Memory Across Model Migration ICLR 2027 · rejected
The problem
Persistent agent memory is exported when a backbone model is upgraded, a provider changes, routing splits turns between models, or several agents share a store; signatures certify that the bytes survive but not that the behaviour they induce survives, so a record one model wrote as history can be read by the next model as an instruction, an authorization or a goal.
The idea
The paper calls this migration-induced safety and privacy inversion and studies it as a property of an ordered pair of models, contributing a formal setting with three executable-outcome metrics, two structural results on what a target-agnostic transform can and cannot preserve, a typed memory compiler and a distribution-free selective-migration gate.
Strengths
The paper measures the full source–target matrix over six open-weight models twice, as a pilot and as a confirmatory replicate on a frozen benchmark with fresh decoding seeds, and reports that the pre-registered gate passes in both runs.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • Records are generator-authored, and target rollouts are shared across sources; the source therefore affects the result through item selection rather than memory production.
  • The paper frames inversion as a property of the model pair, yet target identity explains 0.92 of the variance, and the pairwise predictor gains only +0.009 AUROC once the target is known.
  • Appendix Table 12 (PDF page 39) reports that among 196 confirmed attack cases, 50 are both accepted by the full defense and still result in violations.
  • the benchmark relies predominantly on synthetic memory artifacts rather than memories accumulated during actual agent execution.

DELETECERT: Counterfactual Certificates for Deletion in Persistent Agent Memory ICLR 2027 · rejected
The problem
Deleting a record from an agent's memory is not the same as deleting its influence: a persistent language agent turns one user artifact into summaries, embeddings, graph edges, profiles, skills, cached plans, messages to other agents and sometimes parameter updates, and removing the original row leaves this derived state in place.
The idea
The paper defines deletion counterfactually, as the deployed agent behaving like an agent that never observed the artifact, with residual influence measured as excess risk over that never-seen agent; DeleteCert builds a typed artifact-lineage graph, a closure that deletes, recomputes or keeps each descendant under a risk budget and a utility floor, and a scoped audit that bounds excess risk under a declared probe distribution.
Strengths
The paper proves that faithful recomputation reproduces the never-seen world exactly, that the audit bound is valid for a fixed post-deletion state, that minimum-cost closure is NP-hard, and that no finite probe set certifies worst-case equivalence; on a synthetic structural benchmark, deleting only the raw record leaves 0.45 excess risk.
What the authors leave open
  • In the language-model experiment the judge is the same model as the assistant; the authors report a verbatim-match measure and a null control alongside it to expose a judge that agrees with everything.
  • The numbers in the language-model results come from three seeds fixed before the study, so how much of the reported ordering is a property of those three draws remains open until measured.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • The method cannot run outside a simulator. Both the audit and the planner need the never-seen agent, which only the twin world provides.
  • In the structural benchmark the never-seen agent leaks nothing, so excess and absolute risk are the same.
  • Table 15 in the paper itself shows that, for the same optimization problem, the exact solver not only achieved a 100% feasibility rate but also had a lower cost than IWC
  • the main structured experiments in Table 14 did not measure utility on real tasks but instead used a manually designed utility surrogate

PROVACT: A Diagnostic Study of Causal Provenance Gating for LLM Agents ICLR 2027 · rejected
The problem
Rewriting, such as summarisation, memory consolidation or cross-agent forwarding, may remove the string that content defences for LLM agents match while keeping its influence, a hypothesised failure the paper calls provenance laundering.
The idea
PROVACT is a split-conformal gate on per-action risk computed over trusted provenance graphs (records of where each input to an action came from), trained towards invariance to laundering, sensitivity to authority and amortised counterfactual influence.
Strengths
In a diagnostic AgentDojo study with a local 7B backbone, PROVACT reaches AUPRC 91.8 against a content classifier's 74.2, and at retrospective thresholds allowing at most 5% false interventions the paper reports pass-through rates of 39.1% against 71.5%, not distinguishable from post-hoc ancestor-MLP and plain-graph controls; the paper reports that the causal mechanism is not supported for this detector.
What the authors leave open
  • The replay-and-oracle labels used to score detection are not independently validated: the planned human audit of a 200-row agreement sample was not carried out, so the oracle's error, which bounds every label in the paper, is unmeasured.
  • The influence head does not fit replay influence even on training data, rewrites succeed less often than own-source originals, and no one-component ablation is resolved.

Language models and NLP (2 papers · CSRankings score 0.39)

Topic description

Large language model code generation usually fixes the target programming language before decoding, although verified solutions to the same task can differ substantially in token length; each task is routed to a language before generation, with fallback to another language when unit tests fail, and on a new verified 3,000-task, 8-language corpus live evaluation on 450 held-out tasks shows a simple domain heuristic reducing proxy tokens by 50.3% at 92.9% pass and a learned selector reaching the highest pass rate, 93.8%, with a 3.7% token increase. Dialogue segments are retained, summarized or pruned by a learned controller that scores commitment alignments, reaching 62.9% judged acceptability at 0.092 of the full-history token cost on a long-conversation benchmark, while a human-labelled diagnostic shows one checkpoint failing 78% of commitment probes.

Papers (2)
LangSelect: Cost-Aware Target-Language Routing for LLM Code Generation ICLR 2027 · rejected
The problem
LLM code-generation systems usually fix the target programming language before decoding; the paper shows that for tasks where several languages are acceptable and checkable by the same tests, verified implementations of the same task can differ substantially in generated-token length, so the language choice is a cost lever.
The idea
LangSelect is a verification-aware router that selects the target language before generation and falls back to another language when the first attempt fails unit tests; it is evaluated both by replaying already verified solutions and by live GPT-5 generation that charges every attempt, including failures and fallbacks.
Strengths
On MultiLang-Bench, a 3,000-task, 8-language verified corpus introduced by the paper, live evaluation on 450 held-out tasks shows a Domain heuristic reducing harness-proxy tokens by 50.3% at 92.9% pass after fallback, and a learned CodeBERT+metadata selector reaching the highest pass rate, 93.8%, with a 3.7% token increase.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • The fixed order saves code tokens, but a terse Python prompt alone saves 41.6%, and the paper does not compare the two in the same experiment.
  • The paper only measures generated code tokens, without considering hidden reasoning tokens, input tokens, or the overhead of repeated calls.
  • In real software projects, the programming language is usually fixed by the existing codebase and deployment environment, so the practical value of this approach seems limited.
  • the paper mainly compares language routing, which allows up to three attempts, against a single Python attempt, without considering Python retries under the same budget.

MACC: Multi-Aspect Commitment Control for Drift-Aware Memory Management in Long-Horizon LLM Agents ICLR 2027 · rejected
The problem
Long-horizon dialogue agents built on large language models (LLMs) must preserve still-active user commitments while working under limited context budgets; the paper studies context drift, an unintended divergence from such commitments, and asks whether efficient memory editing also preserves them.
The idea
Multi-Aspect Commitment Control (MACC) scores historical dialogue segments with several projection-based commitment alignments and learns to retain, summarize or prune each segment, using a response-consistency proxy computed by an encoder separate from the generator.
Strengths
With a shared Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct generator, MACC reaches 62.9% judge-rated acceptability on LoCoMo at 0.092 of the Full-History token cost, while a No-History control reaches 60.4% at 0.087; on a human-labelled diagnostic of 60 probes, three heuristic memory methods fail on 55–67% of probes and one MACC checkpoint fails on 78% with no dialogue history retained.
What the authors leave open
  • The rubric-based gpt-4o-mini judge detected none of 13 human-adjudicated stale cases (nominal Krippendorff's α = 0.486 against final human labels).
  • Full certification of the ACCST commitment benchmark is left to future work: reliability of the FORGOTTEN label is below threshold, six-class agreement in the transfer pilot is low, the transfer covers only 14 of 20 planned bases, and the adaptive-yield analysis is incomplete.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • The controller learns to discard memory, and the headline metric cannot detect this.
  • The reward is a plausible cause of the collapse: it combines a cost penalty, a heuristic q_train, and a drift term that the authors acknowledge measures response dissimilarity rather than commitment error
  • The multi-aspect heads are untrained in the main runs.
  • All LoCoMo results come from two test conversations (A.1), and the three-seed t-intervals (±4–7.6) overlap for most methods.

Datasets, benchmarks, evaluation (1 paper · CSRankings score 0.33)

Topic description

A controlled replay on two low-resource African-language classification tasks in four languages, with seven selectors, tests whether synthetic examples rated highly by an LLM judge help downstream learning: the selector with the highest judged label correctness (0.904 versus 0.767 for naive selection) is outperformed on downstream Macro-F1 by another selector (0.202 versus 0.163), and retention constraints such as label quotas explain more of the differences than a consistent ranking advantage.

When Audit Quality Fails to Predict Downstream Utility: Synthetic-Data Selection for Low-Resource Classification EMNLP 2026 · rejected
The problem
Quality-aware selection of synthetic training data assumes that examples rated highly by an LLM judge will help downstream learning; the paper tests this assumption for low-resource classification.
The idea
A controlled replay across two low-resource African-language classification tasks, four languages and seven selectors, six of which retain 64 examples per task–language cell, comparing the judge-audited quality of each retained set with the Macro-F1 of a model trained on it.
Strengths
The paper reports that CoSDA-V2 yields the highest judged label correctness (0.904 versus 0.767 for naive selection) and the lowest shortcut score, yet AlpaGasus leads it on downstream Macro-F1 in both the main replay (0.202 versus 0.163) and a three-seed refit (0.193 versus 0.157), and that retention constraints account for more of the observed differences than a consistent ranking advantage.
What the authors leave open
  • Adding the audit's strict gate changes both pool composition and training-set size, so that comparison measures their joint effect rather than the gate alone.
  • The fine-tunes plus the judge audit cost roughly one GPU-week per seed.
