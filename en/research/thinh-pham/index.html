Research directions: Thinh Pham

Thinh Pham (VinUni) · All professors

Prof. Thinh's paper studies how to evaluate language models before they are used in safety-critical operations. Work on consequence-aware evaluation of language models uses air traffic control to measure how far standard semantic scores overstate operational reliability.

Open challenges. Work on safety-focused evaluation of language models leaves open how to close the gap between semantic scores and operational safety, which risk-aware fine-tuning only narrows.

Topic description

Controller-pilot communication in air traffic control (ATC) tolerates almost no errors, yet errors with very different consequences can score similarly under F1. The paper builds a diagnostic ATC benchmark grounded in aviation standards and feedback from 40 controllers in three countries and scores model outputs by operational consequence. Across 8 models it reports that conventional scores give substantially higher performance estimates than consequence-aware evaluation, and that risk-aware fine-tuning narrows but does not close this gap.

Papers (1)
Beyond Semantic Accuracy: Consequence-Aware Evaluation for Safety-Critical Language Understanding EMNLP 2026 · accepted
The problem
In safety-critical settings such as air traffic control (ATC), a language model can score well on semantic metrics like F1 while misreading an altitude, dropping an execution condition or confusing a callsign, errors whose operational consequences differ sharply; the paper asks whether semantic scores misstate operational reliability.
The idea
The paper proposes consequence-aware evaluation, which weights errors by their operational consequence, and instantiates it in a controlled diagnostic ATC benchmark grounded in aviation standards and feedback from air traffic controllers; it also tests risk-aware fine-tuning.
Strengths
Evaluating 8 models on a benchmark built with feedback from 40 air traffic controllers in three countries, the paper reports a systematic gap in which conventional scores estimate substantially higher performance than consequence-aware evaluation, and it releases data, annotation schemas, evaluation code and prompts.
What the authors leave open
  • The slot weights and the Task 2 error taxonomy are specific to ATC; adapting them to other safety-critical domains requires new expert input.
  • The fine-tuning study uses a single 8B open-weight backbone, so the effects of model scale and training data remain open; risk-aware fine-tuning narrows but does not close the semantic-safety gap.
  • The authors propose pairing stronger models with multi-layer safety mechanisms such as abstention, independent cross-checking, human review and runtime monitoring.
