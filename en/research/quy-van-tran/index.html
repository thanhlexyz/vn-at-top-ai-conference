Research directions: Quy Van Tran

Quy Van Tran (HCMUS) · All professors

Prof. Quy's papers ask when a conclusion from a model evaluation is actually supported by its evidence. Work on auditing modality-importance claims from attribution scores, using statistical inference across several plausible ways of removing a modality. Work on evaluating endpoints of asymmetric training in multi-agent reinforcement learning by resuming all learning and comparing with continued co-adaptation.

Open challenges. Work on auditing modality-importance claims leaves open results beyond one dataset and predictor, and effects such as feature scale and covariance. Work on evaluating endpoints of asymmetric training leaves open comparison with prior methods, generality beyond one toy market-making testbed, and richer market mechanisms.

Papers (1)
When Same Sign Is Not Enough: Auditing Modality-Dominance Claims ICLR 2027 · rejected
The problem
Explanations of multimodal predictors are often summarised as claims that one input modality (for example numeric features) dominates another (for example text); the paper asks whether such a directional claim stays statistically resolved across a declared family of plausible ways of removing a modality, since removal semantics and estimation uncertainty can each change explanations.
The idea
The paper audits a fixed text–numeric predictor under three removal operators, subtracts an operator-specific empirical no-signal reference from the observed reliance gap, and uses paired inference that propagates dependent-sample and finite-null-bank uncertainty; the underlying attribution score is unchanged.
Strengths
In a leakage-audited financial forecasting stress test, the abstract reports that all 21 operator–measurement point estimates favour numeric reliance, yet only 5 of 7 measurements are resolved across the full operator family, while a prespecified marginal-versus-regime-matched contrast excludes zero on all seven.
What the authors leave open
  • Featurewise scale, covariance, support, angular and model-direction effects are left open.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • The paper’s experimental focus is on a single dataset and a single logistic predictor.
  • I would like to see how that count moves with the block length (Appendix A.1), the confidence level and the null subsample size
  • It is well known that permutation approaches are not really meaningful when the goal is to learn something about the data / the model's behavior on realistic data
  • The 5/7 versus 2/7 number is basically a thresholding effect, and the paper reads the wrong pattern out of it.

Papers (1)
Restoring Co-Adaptation: An Endpoint Audit of Asymmetric Training in Multi-Agent Market Making ICLR 2027 · rejected
The problem
When the endpoint of asymmetric multi-agent training (some agents frozen while others learn) is evaluated with all policies frozen, the evaluation switches off the learning responses whose suppression helped shape that endpoint, so an outcome that looks stable when frozen may change once agents adapt again.
The idea
The paper separates frozen persistence from co-adaptive response stability and introduces a restoration audit: resume all suppressed update channels from the learned endpoint and compare the change in a system statistic with a same-budget continuation from a co-adaptive (simultaneously trained) reference.
Strengths
In a three-agent market-making testbed, the abstract reports that staged train-and-freeze training raises the mean minimum quoted spread (MQS) by 0.02854 over simultaneous co-adaptation (20/20 seeds positive), that after 600 restored episodes the mean recovery fraction is 0.9953, and that the matched simultaneous continuation drifts by 6.67×10^-5.
Questions raised by reviewers
  • No cross domain analysis beyond the toy testbed environment is conducted.
  • Many market mechanisms important for studying market making, such as those related to limited order book, are missing from the testbed.
  • Is this something we should generally expect from asymmetric training? Or does it depend on the particular game, the learning algorithm, or the way the training stages are arranged?
  • For example, does this audit tell us something that we would otherwise miss when evaluating existing MARL methods? Would it change which algorithm we consider better?
